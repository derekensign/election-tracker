        How the needle works

        Each needle is the chance that candidate finishes ahead once every vote is counted, from the votes counted so far. With nothing counted it starts from the polls model on the Texas statewide tab, moved by the early-vote turnout mix: early ballots came from counties that voted about 2.4 points bluer in 2022 than that year's electorate did, and the needle adds half of that, since likely-voter polls already catch some of it. As counties report, a model compares each county's count with what it expected there, given the county's usual lean (a blend of the 2022 governor, 2024 president, and 2018 Senate results, shifted to match the final polling average). It learns from those differences how far the state is running from the polls, how each region is running from the state (big cities, suburbs, midsize cities, the Rio Grande Valley and border, and rural Texas), how much of each county's 2022-to-2024 shift is reversing, and how differently Election Day votes are breaking from early votes. Then it projects the votes not yet counted.

        Expected votes start from each county's early-vote turnout, which the Secretary of State already published, plus Election Day votes at 35% of early votes, learned by region as counties finish. Early votes and Election Day votes are modeled separately, because they often lean differently, and a county's partial count is treated as a noisy sample until it is complete. A fat-tailed (Student-t) statewide error and an extra cushion for patterns the model cannot see keep it from being sure too early. Tested on simulated nights, it is slightly cautious: when it said 75%, the leader won about 85% of the time.

        Returns are the Secretary of State's unofficial county results (the same feed as results.texas-election.com), read every two minutes by a recorder that keeps this chart's history; if the recorder falls behind, your browser reads the state's feed directly and runs the same model. Nothing here is a race call.

            How these numbers work

            The state's feed can run days behind for big counties: on the morning after day 1 it showed Harris with 4 in-person votes while the Harris County Clerk reported 104,659. So the tracker also reads the published daily turnout reports of 13 big counties (Harris, Tarrant, Bexar, Travis, Collin, El Paso, Fort Bend, Hidalgo, Montgomery, Cameron, Bell, Webb, and Ellis) and uses a county's own count whenever it is ahead of the state's. Only aggregate counts are read, never voter lists. Dallas, Denton, Williamson, and several other counties block automated access to their sites, so they stay on the state feed.

            Counts are unofficial and arrive on each county's own schedule, mostly overnight, so a day counts once it is over. A big county counts as having posted a day once its 2026 in-person total reaches a quarter of its 2018 total for the same day; below that it is listed as not posted yet rather than read as a collapse. Mail ballots get the same rule separately, since many counties post in-person votes before mail. A county that has uploaded only part of a day can still look like a drop until the rest arrives.

            2018 numbers come from the Secretary of State's 2018 daily reports, which covered only the 30 counties with the most registered voters. Days line up by in-person early-voting day: day 1 was October 22 in 2018 and October 19 in 2026. Those counties have many more registered voters now, so the tab also gives the change per registered voter. The state's daily reports for 2022 and 2024 are no longer online, so those years are compared day by day only for the big counties whose own daily reports are still published: Harris (2022 only), Tarrant, El Paso, Montgomery (2022 only), Hidalgo, Cameron, and Ellis (2024 only). 2024 was a presidential year, so its turnout runs higher than any midterm.

            Texas has no party registration, so nobody can count early ballots by party. The bluer-or-redder check scores each county's ballots with its margin in a past race (2024 president, 2022 governor, 2018 U.S. Senate) and compares that with the same counties' result that year. It says nothing about individual voters, and it waits until counties holding four in five registered voters have posted in-person votes.

            Turnout or persuasion? Both, and switching mattered more

            Race:

            How the split works: the exit poll asked each voter how they voted in 2024. Comparing that recalled vote with the real 2024 result shows how different this year's electorate was (turnout). Comparing it with the same people's 2026 vote shows how many changed sides (switching). Adding voters who skipped 2024 gives the poll's margin, and the last step reconciles it with the count. People misremember past votes, and exit polls are weighted to match the count, so read the parts as approximate. The poll is clear that both mattered.

            Calls and big moves

              All 35 races

              Closest races first. The challenger is the Democrat, or the independent where the Democrat stepped aside (Nebraska, Idaho, South Dakota); margins are the challenger minus the Republican. Counts come from the states' own results files where the site can read them, and Texas from the Texas needle. They're shown as they come in: states count early, mail, and Election Day votes in different orders, so a partial count can mislead, and the projection and chance move on calls (and, in Texas, on the needle). Click a race for its votes and for why its chance moved.

              How this works

              There is no free live feed of every state's returns, so control runs on race calls: the networks' calls as posted to Wikipedia's 2026 Senate elections page, read every two minutes, plus corrections the site can make by hand. Texas, where the site reads the Secretary of State's count directly, uses the live Texas needle. Where a state publishes an official results file the site can read, the table shows its count as it comes in, with the share counted estimated against 2022's turnout plus a tenth. Where it doesn't (or its file fails), the table shows the unofficial count Wikipedia's editors post on the race's page from news reports, marked as such. Counts are shown, not modeled.

              With nothing called, each race starts at its chance from the polls model on the U.S. Senate tab. Unlike that tab's control figure, this one treats a polling miss as shared by every state (one national swing, plus each state's own error), so it is less certain going in: a miss that sinks one party's candidate in Iowa would likely hurt it in Maine too. Each call then tells the model which side of zero that race's margin landed on, which shifts its estimate of the national swing and every race still out. Democrats need 51 seats for control, counting Sanders and King; at 50–50, Vice President Vance breaks ties for Republicans. Independents who win (in Nebraska, Idaho, or South Dakota) are counted as neither party's.

              Calls on Wikipedia are posted by volunteer editors as networks make them and are occasionally early or wrong; a withdrawn call counts as uncalled again. Nothing here is a call of its own.

                Sources and method
                
    
                  
    
                  Polls model. Each race's quality-weighted polling average (D − R margin) is turned into a win probability with a Student-t error model (5 degrees of freedom, so misses of 2–3σ are not treated as freakish). σ = √(5.5² + (0.05 × days to election)² + 3²/effective polls): 5.5 points is roughly the historical root-mean-square error of final statewide polling averages, the time term adds ~1.5 points a month out, and the sparsity term widens thinly polled races. Pollster quality comes from FiveThirtyEight's pollster ratings (vendored copy, 2024 methodology); unrated pollsters get half weight. Races with no polls fall back to a forecaster-rating prior and say so. Prediction-market odds are not used anywhere on this site.
                  

                  Texas Legislature model. Every seat starts from its district's 2024 presidential margin, shifted by three-quarters of the statewide swing implied by today's environment (a 60/40 blend of the legislative generic-ballot polling average and the mean of the down-ballot statewide race averages, measured against Trump's 13.7-point 2024 margin), plus 3 points for an incumbent who is running again. Uncertainty has two parts: a shared statewide error (Student-t, scale 4.5) and an independent district error (scale 6). Where State Navigate rates a district, its implied probability is averaged with the fundamentals in log-odds. Chamber control is the probability that Democratic seats reach the majority line, integrating over the statewide error. No district-level public polls exist yet; if any appear on the race pages they will be folded in.
                  

                  U.S. House model. Forecasters publish ratings for about 144 competitive districts (Cook, Inside Elections, Sabato, The Economist, Split Ticket, Decision Desk HQ, FiftyPlusOne, Fox, Silver Bulletin, RealClearPolitics). Each rated seat's consensus rating becomes an implied margin (Tossup 0, Tilt 2.5, Lean 5.5, Likely 10, Safe 17), averaged half-and-half with the district's polling average where at least two district polls exist (so far only in Texas). Seats outside the list stay with the party that holds them; the three vacancies are left out. Uncertainty is a shared national error (Student-t, scale 3.5) plus independent district error (scale 5.5); control is the probability Democrats reach 218.
                  

                  Seat distributions are Poisson-binomial over the races in a chamber and assume independence beyond the shared statewide or national error term, so they understate the chance of a uniform swing. Ratings tables, poll tables, and legislative district tables are read from Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0), which cites each forecaster and pollster; polls also come from VoteHub's public feed. District boundaries: Texas Legislative Council plans H2316, S2168, and C2333. Not affiliated with any campaign or forecaster.
                  
  
                  

                  

                  

  
                  
  
                  Polls aren't votes.
                  
  
                  A poll is a snapshot of the people who answered a survey. The only numbers that decide anything are ballots. In the 2022 midterm, about 46 percent of registered Texans voted. A race that looks settled in the polls is settled by whoever shows up.
